Portable power stations vs a proper dual battery setup

By Kiran and MitchUpdated October 20267 minute readHow we make money

The short version

  • A portable power station is easy and needs no install, but charging it from the car's cigarette socket is very slow, often around 100W.
  • A 12V power box (battery plus DC-DC charger in one box) charges several times faster while you drive and can still move between cars.
  • A fixed dual battery install costs the most and stays with one vehicle, but it suits regular trips and bigger setups best.

There are three main ways to run a fridge, lights and phones while camping. All three can work. The right one depends on how often you go, how much you drive, and whether you swap between cars.

The three options, in plain English

1. Portable power stations. Think EcoFlow, Jackery or Bluetti. It's a lithium battery in a plastic case with an inverter built in. An inverter turns 12V battery power into 240V household power, so you get normal power points on the front, plus USB and 12V outlets. You charge it from a wall socket, solar panels or the car's cigarette socket. Its size is given in watt-hours (Wh), a measure of stored energy.

2. 12V power boxes. Think iTechworld GoFurther, Kings BatBlock or REDARC GoBlock. It's a 12V lithium battery in a box with a DC-DC charger built in. A DC-DC charger is a smart charger that takes power from your car's alternator (the part that makes electricity when the engine runs) and feeds it into the second battery properly. Most have a solar input too, but usually no 240V outlets unless you add an inverter.

3. A fixed dual battery install. A second battery mounted in a tray under the bonnet, in the tub or in the van, with a DC-DC charger and heavy cables wired permanently to your starting battery. See 4WD dual battery basics for how it works.

Gotcha 1: the cigarette socket is a drinking straw

A car's 12V cigarette socket wasn't built for heavy charging. Power station makers know this and limit how much they draw from it. EcoFlow's DELTA 2 Max manual lists car charging at 12V or 24V, 8A by default. Jackery lists its Explorer 1000 at 7.5A. At 12V, that's only about 90 to 100W.

Jackery says the Explorer 1000 (about 1,000Wh) takes about 14 hours to charge from the car. Two hours of driving at about 90W puts back about 180Wh (about 14Ah), only around half a day's use for a medium fridge. So on a trip, the power station slowly runs flat.

Compare that to a 40A DC-DC charger in a power box or fixed system. Using the same maths as our Power Planner (about 90% of the label), two hours of driving puts back about 72Ah, or roughly 900Wh. That's like filling a bucket with a hose instead of a drinking straw.

There are ways around it. Some power stations take faster 12V input through a separate port, and EcoFlow sells an 800W alternator charger that it says is 8 times faster than a normal car outlet. But that needs heavy cables wired to your starting battery, so it's no longer a "no install" option, and the cost adds up.

Also check whether your socket stays on with the engine off. If it does, the power station can flatten your starting battery. Your car's manual will say.

Gotcha 2: Wh and Ah are different units

Power stations are sold in watt-hours (Wh). 12V batteries are sold in amp-hours (Ah). Both describe stored energy, just in different units, like litres and cups.

To compare them: Ah = Wh ÷ 12.8. (Lithium batteries sit at about 12.8V. Divide by 12 for a quick rough guess.)

  • A 1,024Wh power station is about 80Ah.
  • A 100Ah lithium battery is about 1,280Wh. REDARC lists its 100Ah GoBlock as 1,280Wh.
  • A 286Wh small power station is only about 22Ah.

Now compare with your fridge. Our planner assumes a medium 35 to 50L fridge uses about 28Ah a day in summer. A small 286Wh unit would last under a day. A 1,000Wh unit (about 78Ah) would last roughly two and a half days with no charging, before phones and lights, as not quite all of it is usable.

Running a 12V fridge from the 12V outlet uses the battery fairly directly. Anything on the 240V outlets loses around 15% in the inverter.

Gotcha 3: 240V outlets tempt you to plug in the kettle

A normal power point makes it tempting to run home appliances, but anything that heats things up uses a lot of power. A 2,000W kettle running for 5 minutes uses about 170Wh, and closer to 200Wh once inverter losses are counted. That's a fifth of a 1,000Wh power station for one kettle.

Many smaller units can't run a kettle or hair dryer at all. The Jackery Explorer 1000, for example, is rated at 1,000W total from its 240V outlets. Check the continuous output rating (the steady load it can handle, not the short "surge" figure) before you buy for a particular appliance. A gas stove is still the cheaper way to boil water when camping.

Who each one suits

A portable power station suits you if:

  • You camp a few times a year, often at powered sites.
  • You mainly charge phones, cameras, laptops and drones, and run lights.
  • You don't want anything wired into the car, or you rent or borrow vehicles.
  • You'd like a backup for blackouts at home too.

A 12V power box suits you if:

  • You run a fridge and drive most days on your trips.
  • You swap between two cars, or might change cars soon.
  • You want proper charging while driving without a full install.

A fixed dual battery install suits you if:

  • You go away often or for weeks at a time.
  • You want a bigger battery, roof solar and an inverter all working together.
  • You plan to keep the vehicle and don't want to lift a 15 to 20kg box in and out.

What the extra money gets you

Budget, mid-range and premium units mostly differ in charger size, warranty and support. In power boxes, the Kings BatBlock25 has a 25A charger, the iTechworld GoFurther a 40A charger, and the REDARC GoBlock a 50A charger and a 50A solar regulator. A bigger charger refills the battery faster on short drives. Pay for what your trips need, not the biggest number.

Quick answers

Can I charge a portable power station from my car?

Yes, but charging from the cigarette socket is very slow, often around 100W, so a day of driving may not fill it.

Is a portable power station or a dual battery better?

A power station is easiest but charges slowly from the car. A 12V power box charges several times faster while you drive and can still move between cars. A fixed dual battery costs the most but suits regular trips and bigger setups.

What's the difference between Wh and Ah?

Watt-hours (Wh) are amp-hours (Ah) multiplied by the voltage. At 12V, a 1,000Wh power station holds roughly 80Ah, so don't compare the two numbers directly.
