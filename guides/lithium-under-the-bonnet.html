Can I put a lithium battery under the bonnet?

By Kiran and MitchUpdated November 20267 minute readHow we make money

You lift the bonnet and there it is: a spare battery tray, right next to the alternator. Perfect spot? For lithium, maybe not.

The short version

  • Usually no. Typical lithium batteries are rated to charge only up to about 45 to 50°C and run up to about 50 to 60°C. Engine bays often sit around 50 to 60°C and go above 70°C on hot days.
  • A few lithium batteries are built and warranted for engine bays. Unless yours says so in writing, putting it there can void the warranty.
  • Put lithium in the canopy, tray, cabin or rear of the wagon instead, with the DC-DC charger close to it. If you want the battery under the bonnet, a lead-acid type is the more common choice.

Under the bonnet looks like the obvious spot for a second battery. There's often a spare tray, the cables to the alternator are short, and it takes up no room in the car. For lithium, though, the heat is the problem. Here's why, and where to put it instead.

How hot does an engine bay get?

Hotter than most people expect. Hema Maps puts typical engine bay temperatures at around 50 to 60°C, and above 70°C on a hot day. Right next to the exhaust and turbo it's far hotter again.

Hema also notes that an auxiliary battery fitted on the hotter side of the engine bay doesn't seem to last as long as the starter battery, which car makers usually place as far as they can from the exhaust.

What lithium batteries are rated for

Most van and 4WD lithium batteries are LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate), the safest common lithium type. Every maker publishes temperature limits, usually separate figures for charging, using (discharging) and storing. Here are some real examples:

Put those side by side with the engine bay. Charging limits of 45 or 50°C are lower than a normal engine bay, and that's exactly when the battery would be charging: while the engine is running. Even the "using" limits of 50 to 60°C are reached on an ordinary warm day.

Why heat matters so much

Heat is like leaving milk out of the fridge. Nothing dramatic happens straight away, but it goes off faster. High temperatures speed up wear inside any battery, so it loses capacity sooner.

With lithium there's a safety side too. REDARC says plainly that lithium batteries aren't designed to be installed in the engine bay because they can't handle the heat. It points to the risk of thermal runaway, where a battery gets so hot it can't cool itself and the temperature keeps climbing. REDARC also says heat shields don't solve it: once the battery is hot, it can't shed the heat effectively.

Some lithium batteries are rated for it

A small number of lithium batteries are designed for engine bays. Hema names the iTechworld 1420CA, the DCS 90Ah Hybrid and the Invicta 100Ah Hybrid Starter as suited to most high-temperature conditions. RV Daily's review of DCS's under-bonnet system lists an operating range of -30 to 80°C.

Two things to know if you go this way:

  • The warranty can be shorter. RV Daily lists DCS's warranty as 4 years for a standard install and 3 years under the bonnet.
  • They may not drop straight in. Hema notes some of these use M8 bolt terminals rather than the usual round posts, so your cables may need changing. RV Daily's install needed brackets bent to fit.

The test is simple: the maker should say in writing that the battery is designed for the engine bay, and give a temperature rating to match. "Tough" or "built for Aussie conditions" isn't the same thing.

What about the warranty?

This catches people out. REDARC notes that battery makers won't warrant batteries mounted in high heat. If your lithium battery's manual says to keep it away from heat sources, or gives a 45 or 60°C limit, and it fails after living under the bonnet, expect a hard conversation about the warranty. Read the installation section of the manual before you choose a spot, and keep a copy.

Does AGM make more sense under the bonnet?

Sometimes. REDARC highly recommends that only AGM or lead-acid batteries be mounted in the engine bay. Lead-acid batteries have lived under bonnets for decades, and they cost less to replace if heat shortens their life.

But not everyone agrees AGM is ideal there. 4X4 Australia says AGM batteries don't like under-bonnet heat, with anything above about 45°C a problem, which is why they're usually fitted in the cabin or boot. So if you go lead-acid under the bonnet, choose a model whose maker says it suits the engine bay, and expect it to wear faster than one kept cool.

An under-bonnet AGM can be a sensible pick if:

  • you have a spare battery tray and no room anywhere else
  • you only run a fridge on weekends and short trips
  • you want the lowest cost and accept replacing the battery sooner.

Remember you can only use about half an AGM, so you'll need roughly twice the size for the same usable power. More in Lithium vs AGM.

Better places for lithium

REDARC says its REDLAB lithium batteries can be mounted anywhere in a vehicle cabin or canopy, or in a caravan or camper trailer, and calls a canopy setup ideal for lithium because it can't go in the engine bay.

General guidance only. Follow your battery's manual for mounting. REDARC, for example, says its REDLAB batteries must be mounted upright on a strong, flat surface, out of excessive heat and direct sun, with good airflow.

Cable length and where the DC-DC charger goes

Moving the battery from under the bonnet to the back of the vehicle means a longer cable from the alternator. Long, thin cables lose voltage, like a long garden hose losing pressure, so the battery charges more slowly.

  • Put the DC-DC charger near the battery. REDARC says its BCDC chargers should be installed as close as possible to the auxiliary battery, ideally less than 1 metre away. If that isn't practical, it says to use bigger cable.
  • Thicker cable for the long run. The cable from the starter battery to the back needs to be sized for the charger's amps and the distance. Your auto electrician will work this out.
  • Pick a charger rated for its spot. Some DC-DC chargers are cabin or canopy only. Others are sealed for engine bays. Check the rating before you mount it. See DC-DC chargers explained.

Weight matters too. Batteries, boxes and cables add up quickly, so check your vehicle's payload. Our 4WD dual battery basics guide covers that.

Quick answers

Can a lithium battery go in the engine bay?

Usually not. Engine bays often sit around 50 to 60°C and go above 70°C on hot days, while many lithium batteries are rated to about 45 to 60°C. Only fit one there if the maker says in writing it's designed for the engine bay.

Will mounting lithium under the bonnet void the warranty?

It can. REDARC notes battery makers won't warrant batteries mounted in high heat, and even batteries built for engine bays may carry a shorter under-bonnet warranty.

Where should I put a lithium battery instead?

The canopy, tray, cabin or rear of a wagon or van, out of direct sun with good airflow. Mount the DC-DC charger close to the battery, ideally within about 1 metre, as REDARC recommends.
