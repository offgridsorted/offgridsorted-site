Does my car have a smart alternator?

By Kiran and MitchUpdated November 20266 minute readHow we make money

The short version

  • Many newer utes, 4WDs and vans have a smart alternator. It turns its charging voltage down to save fuel, which can leave a second battery half charged.
  • Quick checks: look for a battery sensor on the starter battery's negative terminal (REDARC's method), or check the voltage with a multimeter after a drive. Around 14V or more suggests a fixed alternator. Readings that sag towards 12.5V or lower suggest a smart one.
  • If you have one, use a DC-DC charger with its ignition wire connected, not a simple isolator. An auto electrician can confirm it in minutes.

If your second battery never seems to fill up on a drive, your alternator might be the reason. Here's what a smart alternator is, how to check for one, and what to do about it.

What a smart alternator is

Your alternator is a small generator driven by the engine. It charges your starter battery and runs the car's electrics while the engine is on.

Older cars have a fixed voltage alternator. It pushes out a steady voltage the whole time you drive. REDARC says a fixed voltage alternator can charge your second battery at 14V or more all the time you are driving.

Newer cars often have a smart alternator, which REDARC calls a variable voltage alternator. The car's computer turns the voltage up or down depending on what's happening. Once the starter battery is happy, it drops the voltage to ease the load on the engine and save fuel. REDARC says this lower voltage is often as low as 12.3V, which is too low to charge a deep cycle battery at all.

Think of it like a tap that the car turns down as soon as its own glass is full. Your second glass, down the other end of the bench, barely gets a trickle.

Why it stops a second battery charging

The old way to charge a second battery was an isolator (also called a VSR, voltage sensitive relay). It's an automatic switch. When it sees high voltage, it guesses the engine is running and joins the two batteries. When the voltage drops, it separates them so your fridge can't flatten the battery that starts the car.

With a smart alternator, two things go wrong:

  • The voltage is too low to charge. Even when the switch is closed, 12.something volts won't push much into a deep cycle battery.
  • The switch gets confused. It decides the engine is off when the voltage dips, and disconnects. REDARC says older voltage sensing "could no longer be relied upon" with smart alternators.

REDARC's own advice is that its simple isolator (the SBI12) isn't suitable for charging a second battery from a smart alternator, and that a DC-DC charger should be used instead.

Check 1: look for the battery sensor

This is REDARC's published test. A smart alternator needs to know how much charge the starter battery needs, so the car has a battery sensor. REDARC says it's usually found on one of the battery terminals, most often the negative (the black one, marked "−").

Open the bonnet and look at where the negative cable clamps onto the starter battery. A plain clamp and cable usually means no sensor. A small plastic box or module built into the clamp, often with its own thin wire and plug, is a sign of a smart alternator. REDARC's support page has side-by-side photos you can compare against.

Look, don't touch. You don't need to undo anything for this check.

Check 2: the multimeter test

A multimeter is a cheap handheld meter that reads voltage. Set it to DC volts (often marked "V" with a straight line, on the 20V range if yours has ranges). The red probe goes on the positive (+) terminal and the black on the negative (−).

This is a simple version of a test published by etrailer, a US towing retailer that sells REDARC gear. We've used REDARC's own voltage figures to read the result.

  1. Turn off the fans, radio and headlights.
  2. Start the engine and take a reading at the starter battery.
  3. Let the engine run for 5 to 10 minutes, or better, check it after a decent drive once everything has warmed up and the starter battery has had time to recover.
  4. Measure again, and do it a few times. A smart alternator keeps changing its output, so one reading can mislead you.
What you see with the engine runningWhat it probably means
Stays around 14V or a little aboveLikely a fixed voltage alternator. etrailer puts this at around 14.4V.
Starts higher, then sags towards 12.5 to 13.5VLikely a smart alternator. etrailer gives 12.5 to 13.5V as the smart range.
Drops to about 12.3V or jumps around a lotVery likely smart. REDARC says smart alternators often go as low as 12.3V.

A rough guide only, not a diagnosis. A flat or faulty starter battery, or a worn alternator, can also give odd readings. If in doubt, ask an auto electrician.

Keep your hands, hair, sleeves and the meter leads well clear of the fan, belts and hot parts while the engine is running. Never let the two probes, or a metal tool, touch both battery terminals at once.

What to do if you have one

Fit a DC-DC charger. It takes whatever voltage the alternator gives, low or high, and boosts it into a proper charge for your second battery's type, whether that's lithium or AGM. Think of it as a small pump that tops up the pressure before the water reaches your second glass. More in DC-DC chargers explained.

Three things to get right:

  • Connect the ignition wire. On REDARC BCDC chargers, the blue wire goes to an ignition source (a wire that's only live when the key is on). REDARC says this is only needed with a smart alternator, and it tells the charger the engine is running even when the voltage is low. Other brands may call this "smart alternator mode" or detect the engine another way. Check your manual.
  • Pick the right battery type on the charger, so it charges lithium or AGM correctly.
  • Don't turn off the smart alternator. REDARC says a Ford Ranger with Smart Charge doesn't need the system disabled, just a compatible DC-DC charger. Tinkering with the car's charging system can cause other problems.

Quick answers

How can I tell if my car has a smart alternator?

Look for a battery sensor on the starter battery's terminals, usually the negative. REDARC says that sensor is the sign of a smart alternator. You can also check the voltage with a multimeter after a drive: readings that sag towards 12.5V or lower suggest a smart one.

Does the Ford Ranger have a smart alternator?

Yes, CarsGuide lists the Ford Ranger as having a smart alternator. REDARC says a Ranger with Smart Charge doesn't need the system disabled, just a compatible DC-DC charger.

Can I use an isolator with a smart alternator?

Not well. REDARC says a smart alternator's voltage is often as low as 12.3V, which is too low to charge a deep cycle battery, and recommends a DC-DC charger instead of its simple isolator.
