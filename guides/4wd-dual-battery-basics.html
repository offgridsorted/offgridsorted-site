4WD dual battery basics

Two nights at a beach camp, the fridge humming away, and on the third morning the key gives you a click instead of a start. A dual battery system stops that happening. Here's how it works and what size suits a typical weekend.

What a dual battery system actually is

Your 4WD already has a starter battery, built to give a big burst of power to start the engine, not to be slowly drained overnight by a fridge.

A dual battery system adds a second, auxiliary (or house) battery for your fridge, lights and phone chargers. While you drive, both get charged. When the engine is off, they're kept apart, so camping gear only drains the second battery.

Think of two wallets: one for fuel money you never touch, one for weekend spending.

Where the second battery goes

There are two main choices, and the big difference is heat.

Under the bonnet. Some 4WDs have room for a second battery tray in the engine bay, close to the alternator (the part of the engine that makes electricity), so cables are short. But engine bays get very hot. One Australian 4WD outlet puts typical engine bay temperatures at around 50 to 60°C, and above 70°C on hot days.

  • Lithium doesn't like it. REDARC says lithium batteries aren't designed for the engine bay because they can't handle the heat. Jaycar, for example, says its lithium deep cycle batteries should be mounted somewhere no hotter than 60°C.
  • Lead-acid types cope better. For under-bonnet installs, REDARC suggests standard lead-acid, calcium or lead crystal batteries. AGM is a sealed kind of lead-acid battery, and many are sold for this job, but heat shortens any battery's life. Check the exact model's rated operating temperature against a hot day in your engine bay.

In the back. On dual cab utes, the battery often goes in the canopy or tub. In wagons, it can go in the cargo area, a side panel or behind a seat. It's cooler, which is why REDARC calls the canopy a good fit for lithium. The catch is a much longer cable run from the front.

Wherever it goes, the battery has to be held down in a proper mount or box. A loose battery in a rollover or sudden stop is a heavy, dangerous object.

Smart alternators: why you need a DC-DC charger

Older vehicles had a fixed voltage alternator, which put out a steady charge whenever the engine ran. For those, a simple isolator worked well. It's an automatic switch that joins the two batteries when the engine is running and separates them when it stops.

Most newer vehicles have a smart alternator (also called variable voltage). It changes its output depending on engine temperature, load and driving, to save fuel. REDARC says it often fails to charge a second battery to a usable level through a simple isolator. REDARC's rough guide is that isolators suit older vehicles with fixed voltage alternators, generally before 2015, and DC-DC chargers suit newer ones. Your manual or an auto electrician can confirm which type you have.

A DC-DC charger takes whatever the alternator gives and turns it into the right charge for your second battery's type, whether that's lithium or AGM. Many also have a built-in solar regulator for a panel. Lithium especially needs one: Jaycar says its lithium batteries need a DC-DC charger with a lithium setting, not a direct alternator connection. More in DC-DC chargers explained.

Long cables and voltage drop

Electricity loses a bit of push (voltage) as it travels along a cable. The longer and thinner the cable, the more it loses. This is called voltage drop. It's like a garden hose: a long, skinny hose delivers a weak trickle at the far end.

A battery in the canopy can be several metres of cable from the starter battery. With too much voltage drop, it charges slowly or never fills up. Two things help: DC-DC chargers handle long runs much better than isolators, and REDARC recommends keeping the auxiliary battery as close to the charger as possible. Cable size depends on the charger's amps and the length of the run, so leave that to your auto electrician.

Solar on a 4WD

A 4WD roof is small and often already holds a rack, awning or rooftop tent. So most people use one of these:

  • Folding solar blanket: a lightweight panel you lay out in the sun while the car stays in the shade. Brands like Kings, Hard Korr and REDARC cover budget to premium.
  • Panel on a canopy roof: fixed, always charging, no setting up.
  • Panel on a rooftop tent or roof rack: some tents and racks have mounts for a slim panel.

A rough rule: real-world solar gives about 70% of the panel's rated watts, times the peak sun hours (how many hours of full-strength sun the day is worth). A 200W blanket on a day with 5 peak sun hours makes roughly 200 x 0.7 x 5 = 700 watt-hours, or about 58Ah at 12V. Winter in the south gives a lot less. See How much solar do I need? for your area.

Weight, payload and GVM

Every 4WD has a GVM (gross vehicle mass), the most it's allowed to weigh fully loaded, including people, fuel and gear. The difference between that and the vehicle's empty weight is your payload, the amount you can carry. The GVM is on the vehicle's VIN plate (the identification plate) and in the owner's manual. Bullbars, drawers, fridges, water, roof racks and passengers all come out of your payload. It's easy to go over.

A dual battery setup adds weight too. A 100Ah lithium battery weighs about 11 to 13kg. A 100Ah AGM is about 27 to 30kg, and you'd need roughly twice the size for the same usable power (more in Lithium vs AGM). Then add the box, cables, charger and fridge. Check your vehicle's GVM and payload, and if you're unsure, weigh it loaded at a public weighbridge.

Want less to install? Try an all-in-one box

A portable power station or battery box is a battery, charger and outlets in one case. A power station needs no wiring, so it suits leased or work vehicles and occasional campers, but it charges slowly from a 12V socket. A battery box with a built-in DC-DC charger charges much faster but needs a heavy lead from the starting battery. See Portable power stations.

Typical starter setups for a weekend

The fridge uses most of your power. Lights and phones add a few amp-hours a day.

Estimates only, for 12V systems in summer. Fridges use about 30% less in winter. Your fridge, the weather and how often you open the lid change these a lot. Lithium sizes assume about 90% usable, AGM about 50%. Solar sizes assume about 5 peak sun hours and no driving.

A 100Ah lithium gives about 90Ah you can use, so a medium fridge setup runs around two and a half days with no charging at all. Two hours of driving with a 25A charger puts back about 45 to 50Ah.

Quick answers

Do I need a DC-DC charger or an isolator in my 4WD?

Many 4WDs built since about 2015 have a smart alternator, so you need a DC-DC charger rather than a simple isolator.

What size dual battery do I need for a weekend?

For a fridge-and-lights weekend, around 100Ah of lithium with a 25A DC-DC charger is a common starting point.

Can a lithium battery go under the bonnet?

It's best kept out of engine heat. If it has to go under the bonnet, choose a battery rated for it and get your auto electrician's advice.
