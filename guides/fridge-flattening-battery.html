Why is my fridge flattening my battery?

By Kiran and MitchUpdated November 20268 minute readHow we make money

The short version

  • Most of the time the fridge isn't faulty. The battery just isn't getting enough charge back each day, especially when you stay put and rely on solar.
  • Beeping or switching off usually means the fridge's low-voltage cut-out has kicked in. That can be a flat battery, the wrong setting, or thin, long cables losing voltage on the way.
  • Work through it in order: charging, battery, cut-out and cables, then heat, temperature setting and the lid. Check before you replace anything.

A fridge that beeps, switches off or flattens the battery overnight is one of the most common 12V problems. The causes are well known. Here they are, in the order we'd check them.

From Kiran and Mitch

Our Hiace fridge, a Dometic CFF45 running off a Kings 115Ah AGM with a 160W Kings flexible panel on the roof, kept beeping. We bought a new fridge. Still beeping. We replaced the battery, and that worked for a while. We tested every connection. In the end we added a splitter on the solar input and a Kings solar blanket alongside the roof panel, and it worked perfectly. The roof panel just wasn't strong enough for how we use the fridge, especially when we stay put. Learn from us: check your charging first.

Quick check: what's your symptom?

A rough guide only. Often it's two or three of these at once.

1. You're not putting enough back in

Think of your battery as a bank account. The fridge withdraws every hour, day and night. Solar and driving make the deposits. If you spend more than you earn, the account slowly empties, no matter how big it is. A bigger battery only delays the day it runs out.

Solar that can't keep up. This is the big one when you're parked for a few days. Here's an example using the same rules of thumb as our Power Planner:

  • A medium 35 to 50L fridge uses about 28Ah a day in summer. Add lights, phones and a fan, and you're at about 48Ah a day.
  • A 160W panel, in a spot that gets about 4 peak sun hours (roughly a Queensland winter day), and allowing about 30% for heat, dust and a flat roof: 160 x 4 x 0.7 = about 450 watt-hours, or about 37Ah a day.
  • That's about 11Ah a day short. A 115Ah AGM gives you only about 57Ah you can safely use, so after four or five days in one spot it's empty. Park in the shade and it happens much faster.

These are estimates, but they show how a panel that looks big enough on paper can still fall behind. A solar blanket (a folding, portable panel, also sold as a solar mat) helps because you can point it straight at the sun and move it as the sun moves, while the vehicle stays in the shade. See How much solar do I need?

Driving that doesn't charge. Many newer vehicles have a smart alternator (the engine's generator, which the car's computer turns up and down to save fuel). REDARC says its output can range between 12.3V and 15V, and when the car decides its own battery is full, the voltage may be too low to charge a second battery through a simple switch (an isolator or VSR, voltage sensitive relay). A DC-DC charger fixes that by boosting the voltage. If you already have one, check it's set to your battery type and wired to switch on with the ignition, as your manual describes. See DC-DC chargers explained.

2. The battery is too small or worn out

If the battery goes flat overnight even after a full day's charge, look at the battery itself.

  • Too small. With an AGM you should only use about half. REDARC advises not discharging an AGM below 50%. A 100Ah AGM gives about 50Ah, which is less than two summer days of a medium fridge. See What size battery do I need for a 12V fridge?
  • Worn out. Batteries lose capacity as they age, and lead-acid types (including AGM) wear faster if they're run down past half again and again. Before you buy a new one, have it load-tested or capacity-tested at a battery shop or by an auto electrician. Forum regulars on ExplorOz give the same advice.
  • Running off the starter battery. A starter battery is built for a short, big burst to start the engine, not for a fridge all night. You need a second, deep cycle battery.

3. The cut-out setting and the cables

Most compressor fridges have battery protection, also called a low-voltage cut-out. It's a guard that switches the fridge off when the voltage reaching it drops below a set level, then back on once it recovers. It's often what's beeping at you. Dometic's CFX3 fridges, for example, show an "ALERT: VOLTAGE LOW" message.

Many fridges, including Dometic's, let you choose Low, Medium or High. As a rough guide, Outcamp puts High at about 11.8V and Low at about 10.2V. Your fridge's manual has the exact numbers.

  • Starter battery: Dometic recommends High or Medium, so the car still starts.
  • Lithium second battery: Dometic recommends Low for an accessory or dual battery. A lithium battery holds a steady voltage until it's nearly empty, and its own BMS (battery management system, the built-in brain) protects it from going too flat.
  • AGM second battery: Low can be too low. By about 10.2V under load, an AGM is close to flat. REDARC suggests not letting an auxiliary battery drop below about 12 to 11.5V. Medium, or a battery monitor that shows how full you are, is safer.

Why the battery says full but the fridge says empty. Voltage is like water pressure in a hose. The longer and thinner the hose, the weaker the flow at the end. Dometic notes voltage drops along the length of a cable, and its fridges measure voltage while the compressor is running, when the drop is biggest. Snowys gives the example of a battery reading 12.5V while only 12V reaches the fridge. That can be enough to trip a High or Medium cut-out with a healthy battery.

  • Dometic says not to use splitters, pigtails or extension leads, because each one adds loss.
  • Snowys recommends 6mm² cable as a minimum for a fridge. Factory 12V sockets are often wired thinner than that.
  • Loose or corroded plugs and cigarette-lighter style sockets are common weak points. Many people swap to a firmer plug, like an Anderson plug.
  • If the fridge runs fine on a short lead straight to the battery, suspect the wiring.

4. Heat and airflow

Heat is the biggest single thing that changes how much a fridge uses. A fridge in a closed, baking vehicle, a ute canopy or a trailer toolbox works far harder. Snowys says a toolbox on the front of a camper trailer can easily reach 65 degrees inside on a warm day, and that a fridge fighting high temperatures will likely drain your battery about twice as fast.

  • Keep it out of the sun. Park in shade, or cover it with a reflective sunshade, without covering the vents.
  • Let it breathe. The compressor pushes heat out through vents, usually on the side or end. Unsealed 4X4's test: if you can't easily fit your hands in the gap around the vents, it's packed too tightly. Your manual gives the minimum gap.
  • Vent the space. A hot canopy or cupboard benefits from a vent low down to let cool air in, or a small fan.

5. Set too cold, opened too often, or a leaky lid

  • Temperature. 3 to 4°C is cold enough for food. Every degree colder costs power. Freezer mode costs far more: on Dometic's CFX3 75DZ, turning one side into a freezer takes it from about 34Ah to about 73Ah a day.
  • Opening. Each time the lid opens, cold air falls out. Grab things in one go, and keep drinks in a separate esky.
  • The lid and seal. Snowys points out that if any air can get in or out, the fridge works harder. Check the lid latches fully and the rubber seal has no splits, gaps or squashed sections.

Quick answers

Why does my 12V fridge beep and switch off?

Usually its low-voltage cut-out has kicked in because the voltage reaching it is too low. That can be a flat battery, a cut-out setting that's too high, or voltage lost on thin or long cables and plugs.

What battery protection setting should I use?

Dometic recommends High or Medium when running from a starter battery, and Low for a second battery. Low suits lithium, but on an AGM it can let the battery get close to flat, so Medium or a battery monitor is safer.

Why does my battery go flat after a few days of camping?

Most often the battery isn't getting enough charge back each day. A roof panel that looks big enough on paper can fall a little short every day, especially when you stay put or park in shade. A portable solar blanket you can aim at the sun helps.
