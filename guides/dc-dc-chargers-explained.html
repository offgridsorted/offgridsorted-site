DC-DC chargers explained: charging while you drive

By Kiran and MitchUpdated October 20267 minute readHow we make money

The short version

  • A DC-DC charger takes power from your car while you drive and charges your second battery properly, even on modern cars with smart alternators.
  • Roughly, you get up to the charger's amp rating back for every hour you drive. A 25A charger gives about 22 to 25Ah an hour. Towing a caravan with the charger in the van, plan on about a third less than the label.
  • Most come with a solar input built in, so one box does the job of two. Pick one rated for where it will live: inside the car, or under the bonnet.

A second battery (a house or auxiliary battery) in your van, ute, 4WD or caravan needs to charge while you drive. For most newer vehicles, that's a DC-DC charger.

What a DC-DC charger actually does

Your car's alternator is a small generator run by the engine that charges the starting battery. A DC-DC charger sits between the car and your second battery. It turns whatever voltage the alternator gives into what your house battery wants, in the right stages, and stops when it's full.

Think of it like a good barista between you and the coffee machine. The machine (your alternator) does whatever it likes, but the barista makes sure you get the cup you ordered.

Two units come up a lot. Amps (A) are how fast charge flows. Amp-hours (Ah) are how much has flowed: 25 amps for one hour is 25Ah.

Why the old way stopped working

The old way was an isolator or VSR (voltage sensitive relay). It's basically a switch. When the engine runs and the voltage is high, it joins the two batteries. When the engine stops, it separates them, so your fridge can't flatten the battery you need to start the car.

That worked when alternators gave a steady, high voltage. Most newer cars have a smart alternator (also called variable voltage), which the car's computer turns down to save fuel. REDARC says a smart alternator's voltage can range between 12.3V and 15V, and that once the car decides its own battery is full, the voltage can be too low for any useful charge into a second battery. A switch can't fix that. A DC-DC charger can, because it boosts the voltage itself.

It also matters for lithium, which needs its own charging profile to fill properly. A switch can't give it one. See Lithium vs AGM.

How to pick the size

DC-DC chargers are sold by how many amps they put into your battery: commonly 20 or 25A, 40A and 50A. The Power Planner uses this simple guide:

121 to 200Ah40AUp to about 40Ah
Over 200Ah50AUp to about 50Ah

Rough guide only. Check the battery range each charger's maker recommends. For example, REDARC suggests its BCDC Core 25A for lithium batteries up to 100Ah.

Bigger isn't always better. Three things limit you:

  • Your alternator. The charger pulls more from the car than it puts in, as some is lost as heat and it has to boost a low voltage. A 50A charger asks a lot of a small alternator. Some, like the REDARC Alpha, let you limit what it draws. Ask your auto electrician.
  • Your cables. More amps needs thicker cable. As a guide, REDARC's wiring kits use cable with 13.3mm² of copper (6 B&S, a wire size where a smaller number means thicker) for its 25A chargers and 21.2mm² (4 B&S) for its 40 and 50A chargers, for runs of up to about 8.5 metres to the back of a vehicle. Longer runs, like out to a caravan, may need thicker again.
  • Your battery. Check its maximum charge rate isn't lower than the charger.

How much charge from an hour of driving?

The simple version: charger amps x hours driven. To stay on the safe side, the Power Planner counts about 90% of that for a van or 4WD, and about 65% when towing a caravan. Here's a worked example.

Say you have a 100Ah lithium battery, so about 90Ah you can actually use, and a medium 35 to 50L fridge that uses about 28Ah a day in summer. You have a 25A DC-DC charger.

  • Drive for 1 hour: up to 25 x 1 = 25Ah, and the planner counts about 22Ah. That's most of a day of fridge.
  • Drive for 3 hours between camps: about 67 to 75Ah, enough to refill most of a well-used battery.
  • Towing a caravan with the charger in the van: about 25 x 0.65 = roughly 16Ah an hour. The long cable through the car and trailer plug loses voltage on the way.

These are estimates. You get less if the battery is nearly full (chargers slow down near the end) or the cables are thin.

Built-in solar: one box instead of two

Most DC-DC chargers sold in Australia are dual input. As well as the alternator, they have a solar input with a built-in MPPT regulator. MPPT (maximum power point tracking) is the smarter type of solar controller. It squeezes more out of a panel than the cheaper PWM type.

So you don't need a separate solar regulator. Check:

  • Maximum panel voltage. Some budget chargers only take smaller 12V-style panels. Others, like the REDARC Alpha, accept up to 48V, which suits bigger panels.
  • Maximum solar watts. If you might add panels later, leave headroom. Projecta lists 360W for its IDC25X.
  • Not every charger has it. The Victron Orion XS has no solar input, so you'd need a separate solar controller.

For how much solar you need, see How much solar do I need?

Inside the car or under the bonnet?

Engine bays get very hot, wet and dusty. Chargers are built for one place or the other, and an in-cabin model doesn't belong under the bonnet.

Budget, mid-range or premium?

  • Budget (Kings, Renogy): DC-DC and solar in one cheap box, often with shorter warranties, fewer published specs or lower panel voltage limits. Fine for weekenders.
  • Mid-range (REDARC BCDC Core, Projecta): Australian brands most auto electricians know.
  • Premium (REDARC Alpha, Enerdrive, Victron Orion): phone apps, solar priority (using the sun first to go easy on your alternator), bigger solar inputs or higher efficiency. Worth it if you're living in it.

Prices change often, so pick by mounting spot, size and whether you want solar in the same box.

Common mistakes

  • No ignition wire on a smart alternator. REDARC says its BCDC chargers need an ignition input to work with smart alternators. This is a small wire that tells the charger the engine is running. Without it, the charger may not switch on. Some brands, like Victron, detect the engine running another way, so check your manual.
  • Cables too thin. Thin cable loses voltage and gets hot, so you get less charge, and it can be a fire risk. Use the size the manual says for your cable length, with the right fuses at both ends.
  • Lithium setting not selected. Many chargers come set for lead-acid. If you have lithium and don't change it, the battery may never fully charge. Check the switch, dial or app.
  • Counting on short drives. Twenty minutes to the shops puts back under 10Ah. Plan solar too.
Get a pro to wire it in. 12V systems store and move a lot of energy. Have a licensed auto electrician install or check your setup, with the right cable sizes and fuses, and never work on 240V wiring yourself.

Quick answers

How much does a DC-DC charger charge per hour of driving?

Roughly up to its amp rating. A 25A charger puts back about 22 to 25Ah for every hour you drive. Towing a caravan with the charger in the van, plan on about a third less.

Do I need a DC-DC charger with a smart alternator?

Yes. Smart alternators lower their voltage to save fuel, so a simple isolator won't charge your second battery properly. A DC-DC charger fixes that.

Can a DC-DC charger take solar too?

Most do. Many DC-DC chargers have a solar input built in, so one box handles both driving and solar.
