Summer camping: what heat does to your fridge, solar and battery

By Kiran and MitchUpdated November 20266 minute readHow we make money

The short version

  • Summer gives you more sun, but heat makes all three main parts of your setup work harder at the same time.
  • Your fridge runs longer and uses more. Your solar panels make less power when they get hot (roughly 10 to 20% less on a hot day, more for flexible panels stuck to a roof). Your battery wears out faster if it sits somewhere hot, and many lithium batteries stop charging when they get too hot.
  • The fixes are mostly free: shade the van but not the panels, give the fridge room to breathe, keep the battery somewhere cool, and pre-cool everything at home.

You'd think summer would be the easy season for van power. Long days, strong sun, panels working flat out. Then the fridge starts beeping on a 38°C afternoon and the battery is lower than you expected by morning. Here's what's going on, part by part, and the simple things that help.

Summer still has more sun

First, the good news. Across most of Australia a summer day holds far more sun than a winter day, based on Bureau of Meteorology averages. In Victoria it's more than three times as much (see our winter solar guide for every state). So over a whole day your solar usually makes more in summer, even with the heat.

The catch is that heat hits all three parts at once, mostly in the hottest part of the day. A setup that works fine in spring can come up short in a heatwave.

1. Your fridge works harder[[d:fridge:72]]

A 12V compressor fridge works a bit like the air conditioner at home. It switches on, cools, then switches off. The hotter it is around the fridge, the more often it runs, and the more power it uses.

  • Fridge makers test at a set outside temperature. Dometic's figures for its CFX3 range are measured at 32°C outside and 4°C inside. On a hotter day than that, expect to use more.
  • Where the fridge sits matters even more than the weather. Snowys says a toolbox on the front of a camper trailer can easily reach 65°C inside on a warm day, and a fridge fighting that kind of heat will likely drain your battery about twice as fast.
  • Dometic lists the CFX3 range as working in outside temperatures from 16°C up to 43°C. Above that, a fridge can struggle to hold its temperature at all.

As a rough guide, we estimate a medium 35 to 50 litre fridge uses about 28Ah a day in summer, with the lid opened a few times (the working is in How long will a 100Ah battery run my fridge?). In a closed, baking vehicle it can be a lot more.

2. Your solar panels make less when they're hot[[d:panel:72]]

This one surprises people. Solar panels love sunlight but don't like heat. A panel's rated watts are measured with the panel itself at 25°C. Every degree hotter than that, it makes a little less power.

  • Most panels lose about 0.3 to 0.5% of their power for every degree above 25°C. The exact number is on the panel's spec sheet, called the temperature coefficient (Pmax). Smaller is better in the heat.
  • In full sun a panel runs about 20 to 25°C hotter than the air. So on a 35°C day the panel itself can be around 55 to 60°C.

Panel type makes a big difference. A framed glass panel on roof racks has an air gap underneath, so it cools itself best. A solar blanket sits in the middle. A flexible panel glued flat to the roof can't shed heat, so it loses the most. Starpower estimates around 18 to 22% on a 40°C day, and more again on a hot metal roof. If you have a flexible panel on the roof, a blanket you can set up in the open is a good summer backup. We compare them in Solar blanket vs fixed panel.

Here's the tricky part. You want the van in the shade to keep the fridge cool, but the panels on the roof need sun. A portable panel or blanket solves this. Park the van in the shade and put the blanket out in the sun on its lead.

3. Your battery doesn't like the heat either[[d:battery:72]]

Lithium batteries handle heat better than they handle cold, but they still have limits. Many are rated to charge only up to about 45 to 50°C. Their built-in protection (the BMS, the little computer inside that looks after the cells) may simply stop charging when the battery gets too hot. So a battery baking in a drawbar box or canopy can stop accepting solar right when the sun is strongest.

AGM and other lead-acid batteries keep working in the heat, but they age faster. A common rule of thumb from battery makers is that a lead-acid battery's life roughly halves for every 8 to 10°C it runs above 25°C. Battery University gives the example of a sealed lead-acid battery designed to last 10 years at 25°C lasting about 5 years if it lives at 33°C.

The fix is where the battery lives. Inside the van, under a seat or in a cupboard out of the sun, is usually cooler than a black box on the drawbar or a ute canopy in full sun. If your second battery is in a 4WD, our guide Can I put a lithium battery under the bonnet? explains why the engine bay is usually too hot for lithium.

Your summer checklist

  • Shade the van, not the panels. Park in the shade or put up a reflective windscreen shade, and put a portable panel or blanket out in the sun.
  • Let the fridge breathe. Don't pack bags against its vents. If you can't easily fit your hands in the gap around the vents, it's too tight. A small fan near a fridge in a cupboard or canopy helps.
  • Pre-cool at home. Run the fridge on mains power the night before, and load it with food and drinks that are already cold. Cooling warm cans on battery power is hard work for the fridge.
  • Open the lid less. Grab everything in one go, and keep drinks in a separate esky if you're reaching for them all day.
  • Set it to 3 to 4°C. That's cold enough for food. Colder costs power, and freezer mode costs a lot more.
  • Keep the battery somewhere cool. Out of direct sun, with some airflow around it.
  • Lean on driving. A 25A DC-DC charger puts back roughly 20 to 25Ah for each hour of driving, which helps on a scorcher when the solar is down. See DC-DC chargers explained.

Plan my summer setup
From Kiran and Mitch

Our Hiace runs a Dometic CFF45 off a Kings 115Ah AGM, with a 160W Kings flexi panel stuck flat on the roof. The fridge kept beeping. What finally fixed it was adding a Kings solar blanket through a splitter on the solar input, so we could park in the shade and put the blanket out in the sun.

Get a pro to wire it in. Moving a battery, adding a panel or a splitter means working with cables that carry a lot of current. Have a licensed auto electrician install or check your setup, with the right cable sizes and fuses, and never work on 240V wiring yourself.
