Summer camping: what heat does to your fridge, solar and battery

By Kiran and MitchUpdated November 20266 minute readHow we make money

Picture it. You've found the perfect spot, it's 38°C, and the sun's been blazing all day. Your solar should be flying. So why is the fridge beeping and the battery lower than you expected?

Because heat makes all three parts of your setup work harder at once. The fridge runs more, the panels make less, and the battery gets cranky. The good news: most of the fixes cost nothing.

Here's what's going on with each one, and what actually helps.

The fridge: working overtime[[d:fridge:72]]

A 12V fridge works a lot like the air conditioner at home. It switches on, cools, then rests. The hotter it is around it, the less it rests, so it uses more power.

The weather is only part of it. Where the fridge sits matters even more. Snowys says a toolbox on the front of a camper trailer can easily hit 65°C inside on a warm day. A fridge stuck in there can drain your battery about twice as fast.

this is the one that catches people out

For context, Dometic measures its CFX3 fridges at 32°C outside, and rates them to work up to 43°C. Past that, a fridge can struggle to stay cold at all. We estimate a medium 35 to 50 litre fridge uses about 28Ah a day in a normal Aussie summer (the working is in How long will a 100Ah battery run my fridge?). In a baking spot, it can be a lot more.

The solar panels: hot panels make less[[d:panel:72]]

This one feels backwards. Solar panels love sunlight but hate heat. In full sun a panel runs about 20 to 25°C hotter than the air, and for every degree over 25°C it loses a little power. Most panels lose 0.3 to 0.5% per degree.

Add that up and here's roughly what you lose on a hot day:

Estimates from Starpower's heat guide for a typical panel. Your panel's spec sheet (look for "temperature coefficient") changes it a little either way.

The panel type matters a lot. A framed glass panel on roof racks has air flowing underneath, so it stays coolest. A solar blanket sits in the middle. A flexible panel glued flat to a roof can't cool down at all. Starpower puts it at around 18 to 22% lost on a 40°C day, and more on a hot metal roof.

we have one of these, and it's exactly why we added a blanket

Here's the catch-22. You want the van in the shade to keep the fridge cool, but the roof panel needs sun. A portable panel or blanket on a long lead fixes it: van in the shade, blanket in the sun. That's what finally stopped our Hiace fridge beeping. We compare the options in Solar blanket vs fixed panel.

The battery: keep it out of the sun[[d:battery:72]]

Lithium batteries cope with heat better than cold, but they have a limit. Many are only rated to charge up to about 45 to 50°C. Past that, the little computer inside (the BMS) can simply stop charging to protect itself. So a battery cooking in a drawbar box or ute canopy can stop taking solar right when the sun is strongest.

AGM batteries keep going in the heat, but they wear out faster. Battery University's example: a sealed lead-acid battery built to last 10 years at 25°C lasts about 5 if it lives at 33°C.

The fix is just a cooler spot. Inside the van, under a seat or in a cupboard, is usually much cooler than a black box in full sun. In a 4WD, see Can I put a lithium battery under the bonnet? for why the engine bay is usually too hot.

What actually helps

  • Shade the van, not the panels. Park in the shade and put a blanket or portable panel out in the sun.
  • Give the fridge room to breathe. If you can't easily fit your hand in the gap around the vents, it's too tight. A small fan helps in a cupboard or canopy.
  • Cool it at home first. Run the fridge on mains the night before and load it with food and drinks that are already cold.
  • Open the lid less. Grab everything in one go, and keep drinks in a separate esky if you're in and out all day.
  • Set it to 3 to 4°C. That's plenty cold for food. Colder costs power, and freezer mode costs a lot more.
  • Lean on driving. A 25A DC-DC charger puts back roughly 20 to 25Ah per hour of driving, which helps on days the solar is down. See DC-DC chargers explained.

Plan my summer setup
Get a pro to wire it in. Moving a battery or adding a panel or splitter means working with cables that carry a lot of current. Have a licensed auto electrician install or check it, and never work on 240V wiring yourself.
