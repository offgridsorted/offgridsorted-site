Winter solar: why your panels aren't keeping up

By Kiran and MitchUpdated November 20267 minute readHow we make money

The short version

  • Your panel probably isn't broken. In southern Australia a winter day holds only about a quarter to a half of the sun a summer day does, based on Bureau of Meteorology averages.
  • As a rough estimate, the same 200W panel makes about 79Ah a day in a Victorian summer but only about 22Ah in winter. Up north the drop is much smaller.
  • Three things cause it: shorter days, a low sun that hits flat roof panels side-on, and long shadows. The fixes: point a portable panel at the sun, park in the open, lean on driving, and use a bit less.

Every May or June the same question pops up in camping groups: "My solar was fine all summer, now my battery is flat every morning. Is my panel faulty?" Usually the answer is no. Winter just gives your panel a lot less to work with. Here's why, how big the drop really is where you live, and what you can do about it.

How big is the winter drop?

Solar is measured in peak sun hours. That's how many hours of full-strength sun a day is worth once you add up the weak morning light, the strong midday sun and the weak afternoon light. A panel rated at 200W makes about 200W only in that full-strength sun.

Daily amp-hours = panel watts × peak sun hours × 0.7 ÷ 12. In the Top End, "summer" is the cloudy wet season, which is why winter there is just as good.

A typical 35 to 50 litre fridge uses about 20 to 30Ah a day (see How long will a 100Ah battery run my fridge?). Add lights, phones and a fan and you might be at 40Ah or more. A 200W panel covers that easily in a Victorian summer, then falls well short in July. Nothing changed except the sun.

Reason 1: shorter days[[d:sun:72]]

This is the obvious one. In winter the sun comes up later and goes down earlier, and the further south you are, the bigger the difference. Fewer hours of daylight means fewer hours of charging. On a weekender trip it also means more hours of lights, phones and fridge running after dark, with nothing coming in.

Reason 2: a low sun hits flat panels side-on[[d:panel:72]]

This is the one most people miss. In winter the sun sits low in the northern sky, even at midday. We worked out roughly how high it gets at noon on the shortest day of the year:

A panel lying flat on a roof works best when the sun is right above it. When the sun is low, the light hits it at a glancing angle and spreads thinly across the surface. Think of shining a torch straight down at the floor, then tilting it: the same light spreads into a long, dim patch. That's your flat roof panel in a Melbourne winter.

The low sun also has to travel through more of the atmosphere to reach you, so some of its strength is lost on the way. Put together, a flat panel in southern winter is working with weak light coming in on a slant.

A portable panel or solar blanket helps here because you can prop it up and point it at the sun, then turn it to follow the sun through the day. We compare the two options in Solar blanket vs fixed panel.

Reason 3: long shadows

A low sun throws long shadows. By our rough maths, at midday on the shortest day a 10 metre tree in Melbourne casts a shadow about 18 metres long. In Hobart it's about 23 metres. In the morning and afternoon, shadows are longer still.

That matters because even a small shadow across part of a panel can cut its output by a lot, not just by the size of the shaded patch. A campsite that was sunny enough in summer can leave your roof in shade for most of a winter's day.

A bonus winter catch: cold lithium batteries[[d:battery:72]]

Most lithium batteries shouldn't be charged below 0°C, and REDARC has a support article explaining why. Many lithium batteries have built-in protection that simply stops charging when they're too cold. On a frosty morning in the high country or Tassie, your panel might be making power that the battery won't accept until it warms up.

If you camp somewhere that freezes, keep the battery inside the living area rather than outside in a drawer or on the drawbar, or choose one with a built-in heater. Check your battery maker's specs for its charging temperature range.

What actually helps

  • Add a portable panel or blanket and aim it. Point it at the sun and move it two or three times a day. Keep the cable run short and use the cable it came with or one an auto electrician has sized.
  • Park in the open. In winter, pick your spot for sun first. A shady site that kept you cool in January will starve your panels in July.
  • Keep panels clean. Dew, dust and leaves all cut output, and every bit counts when the sun is weak.
  • Lean on driving. A 25A DC-DC charger puts back roughly 20 to 25Ah for each hour of driving. A short drive mid-trip can make up for a grey day. See DC-DC chargers explained.
  • Use a bit less. Your fridge is usually the biggest user. The good news is it tends to work less in cold weather. Our planner assumes a fridge uses about 30% less in winter, but that's our estimate and it varies a lot. Set it no colder than you need and keep it out of the sun.
  • Plug in or top up. A powered site every few days, or a small inverter generator for long grey spells, takes the pressure off.
  • Have enough battery for a grey day or two. A bigger battery won't make more power, but it can carry you through a cloudy spell until the sun comes back. Our guide on battery size for a 12V fridge shows how to work it out.

How much solar for winter, then?

Use winter numbers if you travel in winter. For a setup using about 50Ah a day, our state-by-state solar guide estimates you'd need roughly 220W in Queensland but about 460W in Victoria and 540W in Tasmania. That's often more than fits on a van roof, which is why so many southern travellers carry a blanket as well.

If your fridge is beeping or the battery is flat every morning and winter doesn't explain it, work through Why is my fridge flattening my battery? It covers the battery, cut-out settings, cables and heat.

Size my winter solar
From Kiran and Mitch

Our Hiace is a weekender. It runs a Dometic CFF45 off a Kings 115Ah AGM with a 160W Kings flexi on the roof. The fridge kept beeping. A new fridge didn't fix it, and a new battery only helped for a while. What fixed it was adding a Kings solar blanket through a splitter on the solar input, so we could put it out in the sun. A flat roof panel can only do so much.

Get a pro to wire it in. Adding panels, splitters or a new regulator means working with cables that carry a lot of current. Have a licensed auto electrician install or check your setup, with the right cable sizes and fuses, and never work on 240V wiring yourself.
